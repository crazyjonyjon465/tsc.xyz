OUTER WILDS
telstarcat.xyz/media/games/outerwilds

It is, unfortunately, time to be that guy. There are only a few things I can say about Outer Wilds to someone who has not played it, which are:
1. Outer Wilds is a one of a kind experience
2. It can only, really be played once
3. Reading anything — anything — about it before playing it, will fully rob you of your one chance to really play it.

The game is $25 on Steam, currently on sale at 50% off at time of writing. It is critically important that you only proceed further if, and only if, you have played the game. You don't have to play it, but you must keep the option available. It's that good.

Attempting to talk about Outer Wilds as my first time trying to really write about playing a game is absolutely stupid. There are a lot of simpler games that mean a lot less to people, and have so much less to capture, and are probably just more fun to write about. With this game, there is just so much there there that it's hard to know where to even start.

My first experiences with the game were... troubled. A little over a year ago, I started it up for the first time, stumbled around flying the ship before landing on the moon. Thankfully for my later experience, but unhelpfully for the time, I wasn't even aware of the timeloop, so when I centered Timber Hearth's wayfinder on the sun, while that damn music was playing, I thought that I had somehow fucked things up. Upon returning to the start of the game, I thought I had just wasted my time, and would need to do that over again.

The next time I played, I went and immediately got myself lost in the Bramble. Somehow, I obliviously stayed alive, and I just spent my time utterly lost and confused, without any context for anything I was seeing. The only record I have from this playthrough is earning the Harmonic Convergence achievement for listening to all radio frequencies at once, but I had yet to find any actual information. Still confused and a little terrified, I didn't pick it up again for a year.

Once I was goaded into playing again, and finally figured out the actual thrust of the game, it became difficult to put down, and I ended up finishing it in about a week. I just couldn't stop enjoying it. The hook of Outer Wilds, for me, is the experience of learning, which is just damn fun. With every new piece of information, it always felt like I knew exactly where to apply it, giving it surprising linearity despite its open-ended structure. I ended up settling into the rhythm of choosing a planet, finding absolutely everything I could get my hands on there, before choosing another one. The Bramble ended up being last, for obvious reasons. I still hate it.

Having interest in physics, finding out about the quantum game mechanics was a relevation and a joy. As far as applying the rules of superposition to macroscopic objects goes, the implementation is near perfect. I quite enjoyed how the meaning of 'quantum' (indicating a finite number of possible states) dovetails nicely with the game's need to, function, allowing only a certain number of locations for each object going by those rules. As someone attempting to become a scientist, that experience is hard to separate from my thoughts on Outer Wilds. The backstory of people who were intelligent, ambitious, and ultimately doomed is pretty resonant, especially now. I went through much of the game trying to figure out how the fate of the system was, in some way, their fault, just because of how much that seemed to make sense thematically. In some way, I was experiencing a kind of meditation on curiosity, where I was experiencing how it could go right, and discovering how it went wrong.
